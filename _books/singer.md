When did we start caring about octopuses? When did concerns about animal welfare, the environment or morality also start to apply to creatures such as octopuses? Does it have to do with their intelligence, their ability to feel pain, or is it the feelings we get when we see them crammed into those breeding farms? The moral implications of octopus farming force us to ask what or who we consider worthy of our attention and moral recognition. In his book The Expanding Circle: Ethics, Evolution and Moral Progress, Peter Singer, a leading utilitarian ethicist, presents a foundational analysis of the moral considerations that underpin our actions {% cite singermoralcircle %}. 

Singer wrote this book in response to E. O. Wilson's Sociobiology: The New Synthesis, which was published in 1975 {% cite wilson1975sociobiology %}. The book made the case that, given the significant progress made in genetics, biology and human behaviour, morality and our ethical systems in general could be understood through a scientific lens, basing them on empirical evidence. This, in turn, would elevate philosophy, and more specifically ethics, from the realm of theoretical thought to that of a science of moral behaviour. 

In response to this deterministic proposal, Singer presents an ethical argument. First, he clarifies the most basic concepts and premises. He begins this argument by recalling the naturalistic fallacy: values cannot be derived exclusively from facts. In other words, it is inappropriate to make a leap from how things are to how they should be. But is there really such an unbridgeable gap between facts and values? Singer argues that scientific facts and findings can inform our judgements about the best course of action, but they do not trigger our actions. In other words, facts cannot provide reasons for action: no fact can compel me to accept any value or conclusion about what I should or should not do. This decision is rooted in my values. To illustrate this point, Singer presents the following example in chapter three: 

I have $500, which I can either donate to someone in need or use to go on holiday with my family. I know what makes me happy: going on holiday with my children. I also know that this money could bring happiness to other parts of the world, not just to me and my children. These are facts. However, it is the underlying values that will influence my decision. From a sociobiological perspective, our genes make us more attracted to those who are genetically close to us, like our family. But will this information determine what I decide to do with my money? This information may explain and predict what is usually done. However, it cannot prescribe or justify these courses of action, nor can it offer guidelines on what we should do. In order to decide which action is best, we need to find reasons. 

But what does this have to do with octopuses, and how is it related to our morality? There may be an obvious biological, evolutionary or emotional basis for what or who we like or dislike. However, it is our ability to reason that enables us to interpret a tail wag or a growl as approval or disapproval. When we ask, 'Why did you do that?', growls and licks become ethical judgements.

The evolution and history of our species shows how we have gradually expanded our sense of community, first to our immediate family and neighbours, then to our wider community, and so on. These levels of prioritisation are known as the moral circle and have been discussed since ancient Greece. Peter Singer illustrates this concept using a passage from Henry Sidgwick that describes the moral obligations of a gentleman in that era. 

“We should all agree that each of us is bound to show kindness to his parents and spouse and children, and to other kinsmen in a less degree; and to those who have rendered services to him, and any others whom he may have admitted to his intimacy and called friends; and to neighbours and to fellow-countrymen more than others; and perhaps we may say to those of our own race more than to black or yellow men, and generally to human beings in proportion to their affinity to ourselves. 
Henry Sidgwick, <i>The Methods of Ethics</i> in {% cite singermoralcircle %}”. 

According to Singer, our evolutionary history demonstrates a genuine concern for the welfare of those whom we deem worthy of our moral consideration. This may be due to our close, intimate relationships with them (family altruism), our expectation of something in return (reciprocal altruism), or their proximity to us (group altruism). However, we have also historically created moral boundaries between 'us' (certain types of humans) and 'them' (other humans, non-human animals, or natural entities). However, these attempts have failed on numerous occasions. For example, we thought we were the only civilised species until we encountered other societies that were equally advanced. Similarly, we believed that only humans had language until other animals demonstrated complex communication skills. Likewise, we thought that only humans had the capacity to feel until evidence proved otherwise. 


So what makes us interpret these actions as judgements of inclusion and moral relevance? Reasons. Clearly, our emotions play a key role in deciding whether to include or exclude certain entities (for example, those that repulse or frighten us, or those that we find beautiful, friendly or cute). In chapter four, Singer proposes expanding this moral circle. According to Singer (p. 120), this circle has expanded from the family and tribe to the nation and race, often driven by emotion and other factors. We are now beginning to recognise that our obligations extend to all human beings. But why stop there? In his book Animal Liberation, Singer argues that it is arbitrary to restrict the principle of equal consideration of interests to our species, just as it is arbitrary to restrict it to our race. There are reasons that demonstrate why all beings who may be affected by our actions should be included in the circle of our morality. This means that all beings capable of feeling pleasure or pain should be included, for example.

And again, why stop there? Another framework for inclusion could consider plants or other natural environmental entities.
We have compelling reasons to act in this way today, but we may have even more compelling reasons tomorrow that would legitimise the inclusion of others. 

As Singer points out, <b>today's enlightened thinking often turns out to be tomorrow's hidebound conservatism</b> {% cite singermoralcircle --locator 121 %}.

Although it may be challenging to include natural entities in inner circles, doing so could demonstrate that, like early humans, we are unable to overcome our limited view of our own time.
