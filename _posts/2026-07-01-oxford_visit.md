## Visiting the NEUROSEC team at the University of Oxford

Between April and June of this year, I undertook a short research visit to the Design Bioethics Lab (DBL) in the <a href ="https://oxneurosec.com/">Neuroscience, Ethics and Society (NEUROSEC)</a> group, working under the supervision of Dr <a href="https://www.kathrynfrancis.com/">Kathryn Francis</a>. This lab is located in what they call “uphill,” in the Department of Psychiatry, at the Warneford Hospital. I have very fond memories of this experience from start to finish. From day one, the DBL team made me feel right at home; they welcomed me, showed me the facilities, and we got to work. Afterwards, we went for drinks and got to know each other better. I was a little nervous at the start of my visit, as I think living abroad can be scary; however, once I had met the team and seen how they worked, all my fears disappeared. 

The DBL team meets weekly to discuss the progress and any obstacles in their projects. During my stay, this was especially helpful because it let us work together to find solutions—or at least better understand the issues we faced. I also appreciated getting insights into how the lab works. The day-to-day nature of teamwork, the variety of tasks, and the sense of unity I experienced within the DBL team are things that I have valued and tried to bring back to Spain with me. 

I am currently in my third year of a PhD at the University of Granada, Spain, under the supervision of Dr <a href="https://moralsciencelab.com/people/">Ivar Rodríguez Hannikainen</a> (<a href="https://moralsciencelab.com">Moral Science Lab</a>). My research focuses on investigating how we make moral judgements towards non-human animals and the environment. During my time at Oxford, I focused on developing and refining an experimental design to understand the moral mechanisms people use when deciding which animals and plants to prioritise. I also worked on the theoretical and methodological framework for a manuscript that Dr Francis and I are writing together. 

My research lies at the intersection of experimental philosophy and moral psychology. I study what we mean by ‘morally valuable’ and analyse the psychological mechanisms associated with this moral judgement. The DBL team works precisely on this. We shared questions and methods—collaborating with them was the perfect fit. 

During my stay, I also had two opportunities to present my work. The first, at the start of my visit to the NEUROSEC team, enabled me to consolidate and reconsider certain aspects of my theoretical background. This allowed me to develop a much more realistic, well-considered prototype for an experiment measuring the effect of choice history on our moral preferences. The second presentation, at a  <a ref="https://www.uehiro.ox.ac.uk/the-philosophical-moral-psychology-lab">Philosophical Moral Psychology Lab</a> meeting towards the end of my stay, helped me to identify which analyses were clear and which required further elaboration. I would like to thank the Moral Philosophy Lab for allowing me to present my work and for including me in their general lab meetings.

While these academic experiences were enriching, I believe that the visit’s value went beyond these research milestones. Oxford itself provides the ideal space for working from different places, especially when one is stuck with a question or obstacle. Some days, I would work in old city-centre libraries such as the Radcliffe Camera and the Bodleian Library; other days, I would simply move on to newer ones like the Humanities Library. I also loved walking through the city’s parks and Botanic Garden, or exploring the city’s museums and colleges.

Our collaboration did not end the day I finished my stay. Dr Francis and I have kept in touch and we regularly meet up to work on this project and discuss future ones. For anyone considering applying for a research visit with the DBL group, my only advice is… learn the bus times to get to the Warneford Hospital because sometimes they would just magically disappear. Joking aside, be proactive in offering your help with tasks—and do not skip the after-work drinks!

Overall, I am very grateful to Dr Francis for her thoughtful care and guidance, as well as to all the members of the DBL and the entire NEUROSEC group. I am also thankful to my supervisor, Dr Hannikainen, for his support and for funding my stay through his project, and to the University of Granada for helping to cover some of the expenses of this visit.

I feel that I have grown in self-confidence. This experience challenged me to step out of my comfort zone and explore a new country, university, and way of working. Unexpectedly (or perhaps not), this led me to develop a sense of belonging and form professional and personal connections that continue to this day. 

I still have vivid memories of the sunsets, the river, and the student atmosphere in the city. It feels as though I was there only last week.
